Completed research case study

Spectral Reliability for Nonstationary Time Series

The project began as a causal adaptive-window problem for local Koopman/EDMD models. Broad tests did not establish a generally superior adaptive-history rule, so the question narrowed: when can a slow spectral object estimated from finite data be trusted, and when does that object stop being well defined?

RepositoryPortfolio summary
Research path

What changed after falsification

Adaptive windows were not the final result

A Lepski-style causal selector was valid but too conservative in the main setup, and broad benchmarks did not show a general advantage over fixed histories. This negative result shifted the project from window selection toward reliability itself.

Subspaces were more stable than modes

Mode-by-mode matching can fail even when the underlying slow invariant subspace is essentially unchanged. Grassmann geometry therefore became the primary representation for spectral comparison.

Reliability ≠ drift

Cross-scale disagreement predicted spectral estimation error in controlled regular-gap systems but did not reliably measure true dynamical drift.

Admissibility can fail

Under non-normal perturbations, the intended real/conjugation-closed spectral cluster can disappear before conditional survivor error becomes large. The object itself may cease to be well defined.

Selected evidence

Results retained in the final case study

Cross-scale reliability signal

In controlled regular-gap systems, disagreement between slow invariant subspaces at different history scales predicted actual spectral estimation error.

Uncertainty

Finite-data risk near a spectral boundary

When the spectral center is known, a finite-sample perturbation law can calibrate failure risk well in the controlled family. Near the admissibility boundary, estimating that center and plugging it into a point-risk functional becomes nonregular: the operator estimate can converge while the point risk remains unstable. Propagating center uncertainty into a risk interval was much more stable, with pooled coverage about 0.936 for nominal 90% intervals in the controlled local family.

Final interpretation

Reliability is easier to predict than cause

A final matched-overlap benchmark asked whether causal observables could distinguish finite-sample instability from smooth true drift. Held-out discrimination stayed close to chance, even though cross-scale instability still contained information about the size of the spectral estimation error.

Scope

The project does not claim a universal Koopman uncertainty estimator, a generally optimal adaptive-window rule, or generic identifiability of drift versus estimation noise. Its contribution is a controlled study of spectral reliability, admissibility, and uncertainty for selected local spectral objects.
